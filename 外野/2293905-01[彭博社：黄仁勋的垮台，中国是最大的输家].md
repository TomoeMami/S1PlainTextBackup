作者：Catherine Thorbecke

标题：China Is the Biggest Loser From Jensen Huang’s Downfall
 <blockquote>黄仁勋从英伟达辞职，或许能让这家由他执掌三十年的公司走出这场由他一手造成的危机。但对于中国人工智能行业来说，后续冲击才刚刚开始。

这场持续发酵的危机，远不止是他被曝与一名疑似中国间谍、一名直属下属存在婚外情这一出惊人丑闻。华盛顿方面会要求查清：黄仁勋以及英伟达，对于自家芯片流入中国一事究竟知情多少。事态前景不容乐观。

据《The Information》报道，黄仁勋和这名被认定为间谍目标的黄乐（Alice Huang）的关系，据称帮助中国企业拿到了价值至少75亿美元的英伟达芯片。她旗下的 Megaspeed International 是东南亚最大的单一采购方，一年多以来就一直受到美方警示。有说法称英伟达曾介入美方针对 Megaspeed 的调查（英伟达与 Megaspeed 双方均否认存在不当行为）。所有这些，都给美国对华强硬派送上了绝佳弹药。后续出口管制大概率会进一步收紧，就连中国企业原本可以采购英伟达芯片的合规渠道，也会受到波及。

从某个角度看，这难道不正是北京一直想要的吗？中国官方多次释放信号，希望加速转向自研替代方案，也出台政策限制合规版英伟达芯片回流国内市场。但技术官员没办法凭空造出大规模量产的尖端 AI 芯片，这需要时间。灰色渠道与监管漏洞，缓冲了断供带来的冲击。这种依赖关系依旧清晰：近期有报道称，北京AI企业月之暗面（Kimi K3大模型研发方）今年夏天拿到了一大批英伟达高端芯片。而英伟达在中国最主要的对手华为，也承认自家芯片产能暂时无法满足国内需求。

这件事刚好给美国上了一课：这是一种效果最差的管制方式。美国一边加码施压，倒逼中国和华为全力自研芯片；一边又留下各种变通渠道，延缓中国自主替代的进程。一旦这条流通通道彻底关闭，哪怕中国长期发展目标不变，短期承受的冲击会非常剧烈。

夹在中间的中国AI企业，在这个时间点迎来新一轮管制威胁，可以说是雪上加霜。虽然技术进步很快，但很多企业还在互相内卷，更谈不上盈利。行业已经取得亮眼突破，但恰恰是需要证明商业化能力的时候，四面八方的约束却在收紧。周四英伟达董事会讨论CEO去留的消息传出时，中国AI相关股票应声下跌。

麻烦还不止芯片这一件事。黄仁勋不仅长期积极游说美国，主张向中国出售芯片，同时也是硅谷内部开放大模型最有力的支持者之一，而开放模型正是中国厂商押注的重点赛道。

今年7月，因为担心美国封禁中国大模型，他在X平台发布首条推文，号召美国科技圈支持开源权重模型。他还为模型蒸馏技术辩护，称这是 “广泛使用的技术”，属于正常竞争。如今很多美国企业和国会议员指责中国厂商窃取美国AI成果，围绕蒸馏技术的争论，已经成为限制中国开放模型的核心议题。

失去了这位行业内最有话语权、愿意为蒸馏技术与开放模型发声的人，美方出台打压政策的压力只会越来越大。这也会损害一部分美国初创公司和开发者 —— 他们依赖中国大模型获取低成本、可定制的工具，甚至用于网络安全防御。长期来看，这会进一步把AI权力集中在硅谷头部实验室手里。但首当其冲承受损失的，是刚刚失去了最强外部支持者的中国AI公司。

还记得之前大家讨论AI毁灭人类的话题吗？这场丑闻爆发距离中美AI安全对话仅剩几周，本就渺茫的合作希望基本破灭。英伟达事件会进一步加深双方猜忌，信息透明度持续下降，最终很可能演变成一场更加盲目的、不计代价的技术霸权竞赛。

听上去像冷战？而这次又是一桩老式间谍案。黄仁勋原本留下的遗产，是搭建起一座难得的桥梁，让AI革命不至于被一刀切成两半。而现在，他留给世界的，可能是一个人亲手烧毁这座桥的全过程。中国，或许会被留在桥的另一边孤立无援。</blockquote>
原文 <blockquote>Jensen Huang’s resignation from Nvidia Corp. may give the company he’s led for three decades a path out of the mess he created. For China’s artificial-intelligence industry, the fallout is just beginning.

The spiraling crisis goes far beyond the sad and stunning scandal of alleged affairs with a suspected Chinese spy and a direct report. Washington will demand answers over how much he and his company knew about their products being used in China. And none of it looks good.

Huang’s relationship with the suspected espionage asset Alice Huang reportedly helped facilitate Chinese companies’ access to at least $7.5 billion worth of Nvidia chips, according to The Information. Her company, Megaspeed International, is the single largest buyer in Southeast Asia, and has raised red flags for more than a year. Nvidia allegedly intervened in a US investigation into Megaspeed (both have denied acting improperly). All of this gives major ammunition to China hawks in the US. Expect export controls to tighten much further — including on the approved and legal routes that Chinese companies have had to access Nvidia hardware.

At one level, isn’t this what Beijing wanted all along? Officials have certainly signaled their desire to accelerate the shift to homegrown alternatives, including through the government’s own restrictions on approved Nvidia products re-entering the market. But technocrats can’t will mass production of cutting-edge AI processors into existence. That takes time. The gray market and loopholes cushioned the blow of a hard cutoff. This reliance is still apparent, most recently in reports that Beijing-based AI champion Moonshot, maker of breakout model Kimi K3, accessed a trove of Nvidia’s most advanced chips this summer. Huawei Technologies Co., the company’s chief Chinese rival, has acknowledged it can’t meet demand at home.

If anything, there’s a lesson on the worst possible way to execute export controls. Washington imposed enough aggression to light a fire under Beijing and Huawei to rally an all-out effort for local alternatives, while leaving enough work-arounds to blunt the transition. If that pipeline closes, the suffering will be severe even if the long-term ambitions remain unchanged.

For Chinese AI firms caught in the middle, threats of new restrictions couldn’t come at a worse time. Despite rapid technical advances, many are still struggling to compete with each other, let alone find a path to profits. The industry has accomplished impressive breakthroughs, but the squeeze from all sides will tighten just as it needs to prove they can pay. As Nvidia’s board met to discuss their CEO’s fate on Thursday, Chinese AI stocks tumbled.

Chips aren’t the only problem. In addition to being an ardent advocate of selling hardware to China, Huang was also one of Silicon Valley’s most powerful champions of open models, where Chinese firms have concentrated their bets.

In July, he used his first-ever X post to rally America’s tech industry behind open-weight AI after fears mounted of a US ban on Chinese offerings. He has defended distillation, or training one model on the outputs of another, as a “widely used technique” and “competition.” The distillation debate has become central to efforts to restrict Chinese open models in the US amid accusations from companies and lawmakers that Chinese firms are shoplifting American AI progress.

Without one of the industry’s most influential voices speaking up for distillation and open models more broadly, pressure for a crackdown will grow. This would hurt the US startups and developers who rely on these Chinese models for cheap, customizable tools and even cybersecurity defenses. Over time, it could further concentrate power among elite Silicon Valley AI labs. But the immediate pain would be felt by Chinese AI outfits that just lost their biggest cheerleader.

And remember when we were mostly preoccupied with AI killing us all? This scandal broke mere weeks before US-China AI safety talks were set to take place, extinguishing already faint hopes for cooperation on that front. The Nvidia crisis will deepen mistrust and reduce visibility on both sides. The result is likely a more blind and reckless race for dominance.

If that sounds like the Cold War, well, look at this throwback spying case. Huang’s legacy had seemed to be one of building improbable bridges in the hope that the AI revolution didn’t have to be so cleanly divided in two. Now, it may be about how quickly one man can incinerate those bridges. And China might find itself stranded on the other side.</blockquote>
