typhoon源自中文的这种说法是比较主流的，在溯源上相比来自波斯语和印度语更加受认可，摘自wiki:

Typhoon may trace to 風癡 (meaning "winds which last long"), first attested in 1124 in China. It was pronounced [hɔŋ tsʰi] in Hokkien Chinese at the time, but later evolved to [hɔŋ tʰai]. New characters 風颱 were created to match the sound, no later than 1566.[7][8] The word was introduced to Mandarin Chinese in the inverted Mandarin order 颱風 and pronounced [tʰaɪ fɤŋ], later picked up by foreign sailors to appear as typhoon.[7] The usage of 颱風 was not dominant until Chu Coching, the head of meteorology of the national academy from 1929 to 1936, declared it to be the standard term.[9][10] There were 29 alternative terms for typhoon recorded in a chronicle in 1762, now mostly replaced by 颱風,[11] although 風癡 or 風颱 continues to be used in Min Chinese- and Wu Chinese- speaking areas from Chaozhou, Guangdong to Taizhou, Zhejiang.[7]

Some English linguists proposed the English word typhoon traced to the Cantonese pronunciation of 颱風 [tʰɔi fuŋ] (corresponding to Mandarin [tʰaɪ fɤŋ]), in turn the Cantonese word traced to Arabic.[12] This claim contradicts the fact that the Cantonese term for typhoon was 風舊 [fuŋ kɐu] before the national promotion of 颱風.[7] 風舊 (meaning "winds which last long") was first attested in 280, being the oldest Chinese term for typhoon.[8] Not one Chinese historical record links 颱風 to an Arabic or foreign origin.[9][10] On the other hand, Chinese records consistently assert that foreigners refer to typhoons as "black wind".[9][10] "Black wind" eventually entered the vocabulary of Jin Chinese as 黑老風 [xəʔ lo fəŋ].[13]
